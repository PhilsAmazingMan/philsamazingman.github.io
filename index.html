This website explains a systematic review and meta-analysis on AI-based conversational agents: chatbots or virtual agents designed to support people with psychological distress and well-being.

Meta-analysis at a glance

Focus of the study: This meta-analysis examined whether AI-based conversational agents can improve mental health and psychological well-being, especially psychological distress, depression, anxiety, stress, and overall well-being.

Sample examined: The systematic review included 35 studies. Of these, 15 randomized controlled trials with 1,744 participants were included in the meta-analysis.

Average effect size: AI-based conversational agents significantly reduced psychological distress (Hedges’ g = 0.70, 95% CI [0.18, 1.22]) and depressive symptoms (Hedges’ g = 0.64, 95% CI [0.17, 1.12]). Their effect on overall psychological well-being was positive, but not statistically significant (Hedges’ g = 0.32, 95% CI [−0.13, 0.78]).

Additional findings: Effects were stronger for generative AI-based agents, multimodal or voice-based agents, mobile apps and instant messaging platforms, clinical or subclinical populations, and middle-aged or older adults.

Introduction

Conversational agents (CAs), commonly referred to as chatbots, are increasingly being explored as digital tools in mental health care. They can support users by providing psychoeducation, facilitating consultations, delivering elements of psychological interventions, and offering social or emotional support. In this way, conversational agents may help complement existing mental health services and address barriers such as limited access, waiting times, or stigma.

Earlier conversational agents were often rule-based systems that relied on predefined scripts or decision trees. Although such systems can be useful for structured interactions, they are limited in their ability to understand user context, intention, and emotional nuance. Recent advances in artificial intelligence, including natural language processing, machine learning, deep learning, and generative AI, have enabled the development of more adaptive AI-based conversational agents. These systems may process more complex user input and provide more personalized responses to mental health needs.

However, the integration of AI-based conversational agents into mental health care also raises important challenges. Potential risks include privacy concerns, algorithmic bias, inaccurate or inappropriate responses, and safety issues in situations involving acute distress or crisis. Because mental health interactions often involve sensitive personal information, the effectiveness and safety of these tools require careful empirical evaluation.

Aim of the systematic review and meta-analysis

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluates the effects of AI-based conversational agents on psychological distress and psychological well-being. It focuses on experimental studies in which an AI-based conversational agent was the primary intervention. In addition, the review examines factors that may influence effectiveness and summarizes how users experience these systems.

Research question

Do AI-based chatbots work?

The authors examined whether AI-based conversational agents can reduce symptoms such as depression, anxiety, stress, and general psychological distress and whether they can improve psychological well-being.

What did the study find?

The meta-analysis portion of the study quantitatively synthesized data from 15 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), encompassing a total of 1,744 participants. These trials compared AI-based conversational agents (CAs) against various control conditions, such as waitlists, usual care, or psychoeducation.

Significant Results

Psychological distress

The primary finding was a significant and large reduction in overall psychological distress among users of AI-based CAs, with a pooled effect size of g = 0.70 (95% CI 0.18–1.22). This is notably higher than the small-to-moderate effects (g = 0.24 to 0.47) reported for older, rule-based chatbots in previous reviews.

Significant Results

Depressive symptoms

CAs were found to be effective specifically for depression, yielding a moderate-to-large effect size of g = 0.64 (95% CI 0.17–1.12).


Inconsistent Results

Well-being

The effect on psychological well-being was positive but not statistically significant (g = 0.32; 95% CI –0.13 to 0.78), potentially due to the smaller number of trials or the need for longer-term engagement to shift stable well-being markers.

Inconsistent Results

Anxiety, positive and negative Affect

Effects on generalized anxiety (g = 0.65), positive affect (g = 0.07), and negative affect (g = 0.52) were also found to be statistically non-significant across the included trials.

When do chatbots seem especially helpful?

Stronger effects

Effects were stronger for generative chatbots, multimodal or voice-based agents, mobile apps and messenger platforms, clinical or subclinical groups, and older adults.

Positive user experience

Positive experiences were mainly linked to empathic communication, a helpful therapeutic relationship, and relevant content. Negative experiences often occurred when the chatbot misunderstood users or responded in an unsuitable way.

What does this mean for the public and stakeholders?

AI chatbots can be a useful addition to mental health support, but they should not replace professional care, especially in acute crises, suicidal ideation, or complex mental health conditions.
For users

First support, not final care

Chatbots may help users reflect, structure thoughts, and practice coping strategies. However, people with severe distress should seek professional support.

For schools & universities

Prevention and orientation

Digital tools could complement counseling services, but they require clear rules, privacy protection, and access to human support when needed.

For policy & providers

Safety first

Important requirements include evidence, transparency, data protection, crisis protocols, and clear boundaries around what an AI system is allowed to do.

Discussion

The discussion highlights that AI-based conversational agents may be a promising tool for supporting mental health care. Overall, the findings suggest that these agents can reduce psychological distress, especially symptoms such as depression, anxiety, and stress. The strongest effects were found for generative AI-based agents, multimodal or voice-based agents, and interventions delivered through mobile apps or instant messaging platforms. The effects also appeared stronger among clinical or subclinical populations and among middle-aged or older adults.

One important point is that AI-based conversational agents seemed more effective for reducing psychological distress than for improving psychological well-being. The authors suggest that well-being may be harder to change in the short term, because it is often more stable over time and may require longer engagement with the intervention. In contrast, distress-related outcomes may respond more quickly to short-term support.

The discussion also emphasizes the role of user experience. Positive experiences were often linked to the perceived therapeutic relationship with the chatbot, the quality of the content, and effective communication. However, communication breakdowns were an important problem. When conversational agents failed to understand or respond appropriately, users were more likely to have negative experiences, which could reduce the effectiveness of the intervention.

Another key point is that conversational agents should not be seen as a replacement for professional mental health services. Instead, they may be useful as an accessible and scalable form of support, especially in contexts where access to traditional care is limited. The authors argue that future research should examine how these tools work, for whom they work best, and how they can be safely integrated into mental health care.

What should be viewed critically?

The evidence is promising, but not final. The studies differed strongly in target groups, chatbot types, intervention duration, and outcomes. There is still limited evidence on long-term effects. Several studies also had methodological limitations, such as missing blinding or dropout problems.

Conclusion

AI-based conversational agents show measurable benefits for psychological distress and depressive symptoms. The evidence for overall well-being and long-term effects is less certain. Their most useful role is probably not replacing human care, but responsibly complementing existing mental health services.

Quality of the meta-analysis

The quality and persuasiveness of the meta-analysis can be evaluated using the MAGIC criteria: Magnitude, Articulation, Generality, Interestingness, and Credibility.

Magnitude: How large are the effects?

The meta-analysis reports a relatively strong effect for reducing psychological distress (Hedges’ g = 0.70, 95% CI [0.18, 1.22]) and a significant effect for reducing depressive symptoms (g = 0.64, 95% CI [0.17, 1.12]). These effects can be interpreted as moderate to large. However, the effect on overall psychological well-being was smaller and not statistically significant (g = 0.32, 95% CI [−0.13, 0.78]). Therefore, the magnitude is convincing for psychological distress and depression, but less convincing for broader well-being.

Articulation: How specific are the claims?

The claims are fairly well articulated because the authors distinguish between different outcomes, including psychological distress, depression, anxiety, positive and negative affect and well-being. They also examine whether effects differ by chatbot type, interaction mode, delivery platform, age group and clinical status. However, the high heterogeneity across studies and wide confidence intervals limit the precision of some conclusions.

Generality: How broadly do the findings apply?

The systematic review included 35 studies involving participants from 15 countries and regions, with ages ranging from 10.7 to 92 years. This gives the findings a relatively broad empirical base. At the same time, only 15 randomized controlled trials were included in the meta-analysis, many studies had small sample sizes, and most were single-site studies. The results are therefore promising, but should not be generalized too strongly to all populations, mental health conditions, or long-term outcomes.

Interestingness: Do the results matter?

The findings are highly relevant because AI chatbots are increasingly discussed and used in mental health care. The results may change how people think about these tools: AI-based conversational agents appear to be more than simple self-help applications, as they showed measurable effects on psychological distress. It is also interesting that generative, multimodal, and mobile-based systems showed stronger effects, which has implications for the design of future digital mental health interventions.

Credibility: How trustworthy are the claims?

The meta-analysis is credible in several ways. The authors searched twelve databases, followed PRISMA guidelines, registered the protocol in PROSPERO, assessed risk of bias, used random-effects models, conducted sensitivity analyses, and applied the GRADE approach to evaluate evidence quality. However, credibility is limited by substantial heterogeneity, risk-of-bias concerns in several studies, limited follow-up evidence.

Overall, the meta-analysis provides promising and timely evidence that AI-based conversational agents can reduce psychological distress, especially depressive symptoms. However, the evidence is stronger for short-term symptom reduction than for long-term well-being. The findings support AI chatbots as an additional mental health support tool, not as a replacement for professional care.
